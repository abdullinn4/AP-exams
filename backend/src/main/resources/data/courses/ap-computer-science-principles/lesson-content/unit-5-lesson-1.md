## Computing & Society – Impact, Equity & Bias

You've learned how computers work, how to program them, and how the Internet connects everything. But here's the most important question of all: **What happens when technology meets real people?**

Every app you use, every website you visit, every algorithm that decides what you see – they all affect human lives. Sometimes for the better. Sometimes for the worse. And often in ways the creators never imagined.

Think about the last time you scrolled through TikTok. Did you ever wonder why that one video appeared? How many hours have you lost to the algorithm? Who decided what you'd see next? And what about people who can't even access TikTok because they don't have Internet?

This module is about the messy, complicated, and fascinating relationship between computing and society. You'll explore how innovations can have both beneficial and harmful effects, why some people get left behind in the digital world, and how bias can sneak into seemingly neutral code. By the end, you'll see that being a good programmer isn't just about writing code that works – it's about writing code that works **for everyone**, responsibly and ethically.



## Beneficial and Harmful Effects – Two Sides of the Same Coin

### People Create Computing Innovations

Every line of code, every app, every website started as an idea in someone's head. People create computing innovations to solve problems, express creativity, or just because they can. But once an innovation is out in the world, it takes on a life of its own.

Think about social media. When the first social networks appeared, their creators probably imagined people sharing photos and connecting with friends. They probably didn't imagine:
- Political disinformation campaigns influencing elections
- Teenagers comparing their lives to unrealistic highlight reels and developing anxiety
- Algorithms that can radicalize users toward extremism
- Data mining that sells your personal information to advertisers
- Cyberbullying and online harassment at massive scale

But all of that happened. And it happened because technology interacts with human nature in complex, unpredictable ways.

### How Technology Changes How We Do Things

The way people complete tasks often changes to incorporate new computing innovations. Think about how your parents or grandparents did things compared to you:

| Task | Then | Now |
|------|------|-----|
| Watch a movie | Rent a DVD from Blockbuster | Stream on Netflix |
| Listen to music | Buy a CD or cassette | Spotify/Apple Music |
| Get directions | Print MapQuest directions (or use a paper map!) | Google Maps or Waze |
| Shop for almost anything | Go to the mall or a specific store | Amazon delivered tomorrow |
| Pay for something | Cash or check | Phone tap (Apple Pay, Google Pay) |
| Meet people | Through friends or events | Dating apps |

Each change brought benefits: convenience, speed, choice. But also new problems:
- Streaming means you don't own the music anymore – if Spotify removes an album, it's gone.
- GPS means you never get lost, but also never develop a mental map of your city.
- Amazon is convenient, but local bookstores and shops close, communities lose character.
- Digital payments mean you never handle money, but also lose track of spending and risk fraud.

### Unanticipated Effects

Here's a key insight from the College Board: **Not every effect of a computing innovation is anticipated in advance.**

Sometimes effects are completely unexpected. When GPS was developed for military use, no one predicted it would enable:
- Ridesharing apps like Uber and Lyft
- Pokemon Go and location-based gaming
- Targeted location-based advertising
- Stalkers tracking victims via hidden AirTags
- Geofencing and location-based marketing

When the World Wide Web was created by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989, he imagined scientists sharing research papers. He didn't imagine cat videos, social media, online shopping, streaming, memes, or online dating. He certainly didn't imagine that the web would become a battleground for democracy, privacy, and human rights.

**Real-life example: The Hashtag.** The hashtag symbol (#) was originally just a way to group numbers on telephones. In 2007, a Twitter user named Chris Messina suggested using it to group conversations. Now hashtags organize movements (#BlackLivesMatter), spread awareness (#MeToo), and create global conversations. No one at the telephone company could have predicted that.

### One Effect, Two Views

A single effect can be viewed as both beneficial and harmful by different people, or even by the same person.

Take **targeted advertising**:
- **Beneficial:** You see ads for products you actually want. Small businesses can reach exactly the customers who might buy from them. Advertisers don't waste money showing you stuff you'd never buy.
- **Harmful:** Your personal data is collected without your full understanding. You're manipulated into buying things you don't need. Your emotions can be targeted (e.g., showing sad ads to people who seem depressed). Privacy is eroded, and you have no control over who has your data.

Or **facial recognition**:
- **Beneficial:** Find missing children, unlock your phone instantly, tag friends in photos automatically, catch criminals in crowds.
- **Harmful:** Governments can track citizens without warrants, leading to surveillance states. False arrests due to misidentification (several cases documented). Higher error rates for people with darker skin, leading to discrimination. Chilling effect on free speech and assembly.

Or **social media algorithms**:
- **Beneficial:** You discover new interests, connect with like-minded people, find communities you didn't know existed.
- **Harmful:** You get trapped in echo chambers, only seeing views you already agree with. Misinformation spreads faster than truth. You compare your real life to others' highlight reels and feel inadequate.

**Real-life example:** A teenager might love TikTok for the creative outlet and community, but also feel anxious about the pressure to be perfect and the endless scrolling that eats hours of their day. Same person, two views.

### Advances in Other Fields

Computing hasn't just changed computing – it's revolutionized everything else. Medicine, engineering, communications, the arts – all transformed.

**Medicine:**
- MRI and CT scans create detailed 3D images of the inside of your body
- Robotic surgery allows precise, minimally invasive procedures
- Drug discovery simulations test millions of compounds in silico before animal trials
- Electronic health records make your medical history available anywhere, but also raise privacy concerns
- Telemedicine lets you consult a doctor from home, vital during COVID-19

**Engineering:**
- CAD (Computer-Aided Design) software lets engineers design and test virtually
- Stress simulations predict how bridges, planes, and buildings will hold up
- 3D printing creates prototypes and even final products
- Autonomous vehicles use sensors and AI to navigate

**Communications:**
- Instant global messaging (WhatsApp, WeChat)
- Video calls with anyone, anywhere (Zoom, FaceTime)
- Social movements organized online (Arab Spring, #FridaysForFuture)
- Global collaboration on open-source software

**The Arts:**
- Digital music production (every pop song uses Auto-Tune and digital effects)
- CGI in movies (Marvel, Avatar, animated films)
- AI-generated art (DALL-E, Midjourney) – still controversial
- Interactive installations and digital art exhibits
- Streaming platforms for independent artists

### Unintended Uses

The CED lists specific examples of innovations used in ways creators hadn't originally intended:

**The World Wide Web** was originally intended only for rapid and easy exchange of information within the scientific community. Now it's where you watch TikTok, order pizza, argue with strangers about movies, take college courses, find dates, and sometimes do actual research.

**Targeted advertising** is used to help businesses reach customers, but it can be misused at both individual and aggregate levels:
- **Individually:** Manipulating people's emotions and decisions. Cambridge Analytica used Facebook data to target voters with personalized political ads, potentially influencing elections.
- **Aggregately:** Microtargeting political ads to sway elections, spreading divisive content to specific groups, suppressing voter turnout.

**Machine learning and data mining** have enabled innovation in medicine, business, and science – but information discovered this way has also been used to discriminate against groups of individuals.
- Insurance companies using data to charge higher rates to certain groups
- Banks using algorithms to deny loans in minority neighborhoods (redlining 2.0)
- Employers screening resumes and rejecting candidates based on biased models
- Predictive policing systems targeting minority communities unfairly

### Harmful Impacts

Some of the ways computing innovations can be used may have a harmful impact on society, the economy, or culture.

**On Society:**
- Social media algorithms can polarize communities, making people more extreme in their views
- Misinformation spreads faster than truth, eroding trust in institutions, science, and democracy
- Online echo chambers reinforce existing beliefs and make people unreachable by opposing views
- Cyberbullying and online harassment can destroy lives, especially among teens
- Addiction to screens and social media affects mental health, especially in young people

**On the Economy:**
- Automation eliminates jobs, especially in manufacturing, retail, and customer service
- Gig economy workers (Uber drivers, DoorDash delivery) lack benefits, job security, and stability
- Monopolies (Google, Amazon, Facebook, Apple) stifle competition and innovation
- Wealth concentrates among tech billionaires while workers struggle
- Local businesses can't compete with global online giants

**On Culture:**
- Global culture homogenizes as everyone watches the same Netflix shows and TikTok trends
- Local traditions, languages, and customs may be lost as younger generations adopt global online culture
- Attention spans shorten as we're trained to consume short, fast content
- Deepfakes and manipulated media erode trust in what we see and hear
- The line between reality and fiction blurs

### Responsibility of Programmers

**Responsible programmers try to consider the unintended ways their computing innovations can be used and the potential beneficial and harmful effects of these new uses.**

This is a big deal. Before releasing a product, good developers ask:
- How could this be misused?
- Who might be harmed?
- What safeguards can we build in?
- What are the ethical implications?
- Are we making the world better or worse?

But here's the honest truth: **It is not possible for a programmer to consider all the ways a computing innovation can be used.** Human creativity (and maliciousness) is infinite. You can't predict everything. But you can try. And you can build in ethical guidelines, privacy protections, and feedback loops to catch problems early.

**Real-life example:** When Facebook launched the "Like" button, they probably thought it was harmless – a way to show appreciation. They didn't predict that it would become a social currency, driving teens to seek validation and compare themselves. They didn't predict that it would be used to train algorithms that optimize for engagement, leading to addiction and polarization. They could have anticipated more, but they didn't.

### Unintended Beneficial Effects

Sometimes innovations have unintended **beneficial** effects by leading to advances in other fields.

- The space race gave us satellite TV, GPS, freeze-dried food, memory foam, and scratch-resistant lenses.
- Military research led to the Internet, which gave us everything.
- Video game technology is now used for surgical training, flight simulators, therapy for PTSD, and physical rehabilitation.
- CAPTCHA (those "I'm not a robot" tests) was designed to stop bots, but the human effort was used to digitize books and train AI.
- Foldit, a game where players fold proteins, led to scientific discoveries about protein structures that could help cure diseases.

### Rapid Sharing Changes Everything

When a program can be shared instantly with millions of people, its impact multiplies. A simple app made by one person can reach the entire world overnight.

**Example:** Flappy Bird – a simple, frustrating game made by one Vietnamese developer, Dong Nguyen. It became a global phenomenon in 2014, making him $50,000 per day in ad revenue. Then he took it down because he felt guilty about people getting addicted and spending too much time on it. He couldn't control how people used his creation.

**Example:** Signal, the encrypted messaging app, saw downloads skyrocket after WhatsApp changed its privacy policy. One person's open-source project became a global tool for privacy advocates, journalists, and activists. Its creator couldn't have predicted that.

The speed and scale of distribution means effects are amplified far beyond what any individual programmer could control.



## The Digital Divide – Who Gets Left Behind?

### What Is the Digital Divide?

The **digital divide** refers to differing access to computing devices and the Internet, based on socioeconomic, geographic, or demographic characteristics.

In plain English: not everyone has the same opportunity to get online. And in a world where everything from homework to job applications to government services is online, that's a huge problem.

It's not just about having a computer. It's about:
- Having reliable high-speed Internet
- Having the skills to use technology effectively
- Having access to devices that are up-to-date
- Having the freedom to use them without barriers

### How Access Varies

Internet access varies between:

| Factor | Examples |
|--------|----------|
| **Socioeconomic** | Rich families have multiple computers, tablets, and high-speed fiber. Poor families may have no computer, only a smartphone with limited data, or nothing at all. |
| **Geographic** | Cities have fiber optic, 5G, multiple providers. Rural areas may have slow DSL, satellite with high latency, or nothing. Tribal lands often have especially poor access. |
| **Demographic** | Older adults less likely to be online or have digital skills. People with disabilities face barriers in websites and apps that aren't accessible. Some racial and ethnic groups have lower rates of home broadband. |
| **Global** | Developed countries (US, Europe, Japan) have high access (80-95%). Developing countries (parts of Africa, Asia) have much lower rates. In some countries, less than 20% have Internet. |

**Real numbers:**
- In the US, about 7% of households have no Internet access at all (2023). That's millions of people.
- About 15% of US households have only smartphone access – no home broadband.
- Globally, about 2.6 billion people are still offline (2023) – that's one-third of humanity.
- In sub-Saharan Africa, only about 30% use the Internet.
- Women in low-income countries are 20% less likely than men to own a smartphone.

### Who Is Affected?

The digital divide can affect both groups and individuals:

- **Individuals:** A student without home Internet can't do homework, research, or apply to college online. A job seeker without email or a computer can't apply for most jobs. An elderly person without video chat becomes isolated from family. A person without digital skills can't access government services or online banking.
- **Groups:** Entire communities can be left behind economically if they lack connectivity. Rural areas lose businesses, young people move away, and the local economy shrinks. Minority groups without access miss out on educational and economic opportunities, perpetuating inequality.

### Why It Matters – Issues Raised

The digital divide raises serious issues:

| Issue | Explanation |
|-------|-------------|
| **Equity** | Is it fair that some kids can't do their homework because they lack Internet, while others have all the resources? Is it fair that some communities can't attract businesses because they lack broadband? |
| **Access** | How can people participate in modern society without being online? Democracy requires informed citizens; healthcare increasingly uses telemedicine; banking is online; job applications are online. Those without access are excluded. |
| **Influence** | Those who control information shape public opinion. If you're offline, you have no voice in online debates, no ability to counter misinformation, no platform to advocate for your community. |

These issues exist both globally (rich countries vs. poor countries) and locally (rich neighborhoods vs. poor neighborhoods in the same city). In many US cities, wealthy areas have fiber while low-income areas just a few miles away have slow DSL.

### Who Can Make a Difference?

The digital divide is affected by the actions of:

- **Individuals:** Donating old computers to schools or libraries, teaching digital skills to older relatives, advocating for better Internet in your community, supporting organizations that bridge the divide.
- **Organizations:** Nonprofits like EveryoneOn and PCs for People provide low-cost computers and Internet. Companies like Comcast offer low-income Internet plans (Internet Essentials). Libraries provide free public access and training.
- **Governments:** Building broadband infrastructure in underserved areas (the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act in the US includes $65 billion for broadband). Subsidizing access for low-income families (the Affordable Connectivity Program). Funding digital literacy programs in schools and libraries. Requiring universal access as a goal.

**Example:** During COVID-19, many school districts realized students lacked home Internet. Some parked Wi-Fi-enabled school buses in neighborhoods. Some partnered with companies to provide hotspots. Some distributed Chromebooks. Governments funded emergency broadband programs. But the problem was already there – the pandemic just made it visible.



## Computing Bias – When Algorithms Discriminate

### What Is Bias in Computing?

**Computing innovations can reflect existing human biases** – not because computers are mean, but because they're trained on human data and designed by humans who have blind spots.

Bias can enter in two main ways:

1. **Biases written into algorithms** – The programmer's assumptions, choices, and values embed bias. For example, if a programmer decides that "proximity to previous crime" is a feature for predicting future crime, that choice reflects a belief about crime patterns that may be biased.
2. **Biases in the data** – The training data reflects historical or societal biases. If you train a hiring algorithm on resumes of past successful hires, and your company historically hired mostly men, the algorithm will learn to prefer men. The data is biased, so the algorithm learns bias.

### Examples of Algorithmic Bias

**Facial recognition** – Studies by MIT researcher Joy Buolamwini found that many commercial facial recognition systems have higher error rates for people with darker skin, especially women. For example, one system had error rates of less than 1% for light-skinned men but over 30% for dark-skinned women. Why? Because the training data was mostly light-skinned male faces. The algorithm learned from biased data.

**Hiring algorithms** – Amazon built an AI to screen job resumes. It was trained on 10 years of resumes submitted to Amazon, mostly from men (since tech is male-dominated). The algorithm learned to penalize resumes that included the word "women's" (like "women's chess club captain") and preferred words like "executed" that appeared more in male resumes. Amazon scrapped the system before it was used, but the bias was real.

**Predictive policing** – Algorithms like PredPol predict where crime will occur based on historical arrest data. But historical arrests reflect biased policing, not actual crime. Police are more likely to patrol and arrest in minority neighborhoods, so data shows more crime there. The algorithm sends police back to those same neighborhoods, leading to more arrests, creating a feedback loop that reinforces the bias.

**Healthcare algorithms** – A 2019 study found that a widely used algorithm that predicted which patients needed extra care was biased against Black patients. The algorithm used healthcare costs as a proxy for health needs. But less money is spent on Black patients even when they're equally sick, due to systemic inequalities. So the algorithm systematically underestimated how sick Black patients were and denied them extra care.

**Criminal justice algorithms** – Algorithms like COMPAS are used to predict whether a defendant will re-offend, influencing bail and sentencing decisions. ProPublica found that COMPAS was biased against Black defendants: it falsely predicted they would re-offend at twice the rate of white defendants. Yet it claimed to be "race-blind" – it didn't use race as a variable, but it used other variables correlated with race (like zip code, employment history) that served as proxies.

**Search engine autocomplete** – Type "why are women so" into Google, and you might get suggestions like "emotional" or "dramatic". Type "why are men so" and you might get "lazy" or "selfish". These reflect societal stereotypes embedded in search data.

### Where Bias Creeps In

Biases can be embedded at all levels of software development:

| Level | How Bias Appears |
|-------|------------------|
| **Problem definition** | Choosing what to solve. E.g., predicting crime vs. predicting police behavior. The choice itself reflects values. |
| **Data collection** | What data is included, what's left out. E.g., using only data from certain neighborhoods. |
| **Data labeling** | How categories are defined. E.g., what counts as "risky behavior" – subjective judgment. |
| **Feature selection** | Which variables are used. E.g., using zip code as a proxy for race, which introduces bias. |
| **Model design** | Algorithm choices, thresholds, trade-offs. E.g., optimizing for accuracy may ignore disparate impact. |
| **Testing** | Who is included in test sets. If test set lacks diversity, you won't detect bias. |
| **Deployment** | How results are used and interpreted. E.g., judges may blindly trust algorithmic scores. |

### Programmers' Responsibility

**Programmers should take action to reduce bias in algorithms used for computing innovations as a way of combating existing human biases.**

This means:
- **Diverse development teams** – Different perspectives catch different blind spots. A team of all men might not think about how an algorithm affects women. A team of all white people might not think about racial bias.
- **Auditing algorithms for disparate impact** – Test the algorithm on different groups and see if outcomes differ. If it's less accurate for some groups, that's a red flag.
- **Testing with diverse data** – Ensure training and test data include a representative sample of all groups.
- **Being transparent about limitations** – No algorithm is perfect. Be honest about what it can and can't do, and who it might not work well for.
- **Building in fairness constraints** – Some algorithms can be designed to explicitly enforce fairness (e.g., requiring equal false positive rates across groups).
- **Involving affected communities** – Talk to the people who will be impacted by your algorithm. What do they think? What concerns do they have?

### Real Talk: It's Hard

Bias is not always obvious. An algorithm might seem perfectly fair – it treats everyone the same! But if it treats everyone the same when they start from different places, that's not actually fair.

Example: A school uses an algorithm to predict which students need extra help. It uses grades as input. But grades reflect past opportunities – students from wealthy districts had better teachers, more resources, smaller classes. The algorithm will flag wealthy students as needing less help, and poor students as needing more help. But that's just reflecting inequality, not predicting potential. The algorithm is "fair" in the sense of treating grades equally, but the outcome is biased because the input is biased.

This is called **disparate impact** – when a seemingly neutral rule has a disproportionately negative effect on a protected group. Even if you don't intend to discriminate, the outcome may be discriminatory.

### Bias Is Not Just in Code

Bias isn't only about algorithms. It's also about who builds technology, who it's designed for, and who benefits.

**Real-life example:** Early voice recognition systems (like in cars) had much higher error rates for women than men. Why? Because the development teams were mostly male, and they tested on their own voices. They didn't think to test with diverse voices.

**Real-life example:** Apple's Health app launched in 2014 with a feature to track blood alcohol content, but **no period tracker** for menstrual cycles. Critics pointed out that a team with more women would likely have noticed that glaring omission. Apple eventually added period tracking, but it took years. The lack of diversity led to a product that didn't meet the needs of half its users.

**Real-life example:** Google Photos in 2015 tagged two Black friends as "gorillas." The algorithm was trained on insufficient data. Google apologized and "fixed" it by removing the gorilla tag entirely (not exactly a fix). This shows how bias can lead to offensive and harmful errors.



## Putting It All Together – Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Social Media Algorithms

**The innovation:** Recommendation algorithms that show users content they're likely to engage with (like TikTok's For You page, YouTube's recommendations, Instagram's Explore).

**Intended purpose:** Keep users engaged longer, show relevant content, increase ad revenue.

**Unintended effects:**
- **Beneficial:** Users discover new interests, connect with niche communities, find news they care about.
- **Harmful:** Echo chambers form, reinforcing existing beliefs and making users more extreme. Misinformation and conspiracy theories spread because they're engaging. Users can be radicalized (e.g., YouTube's algorithm has been shown to lead users down alt-right rabbit holes). Anxiety and depression increase from social comparison. Addiction to endless scrolling wastes time.

**Bias:** Algorithms may amplify certain voices while silencing others. Content moderation can be biased against minorities (e.g., studies show Black creators' content is more likely to be flagged or demonetized). The algorithm optimizes for engagement, which often means showing extreme, controversial, or divisive content.

**Digital divide:** Those without access miss out on community and information, but also miss the harms. Some argue that not being on social media is a privilege.

### Case Study 2: Online Education Platforms

**The innovation:** Khan Academy, Coursera, edX, YouTube tutorials – free or low-cost education online.

**Intended purpose:** Democratize education, make learning accessible to all, allow anyone to learn anything.

**Unintended effects:**
- **Beneficial:** Anyone with Internet can learn almost anything for free. People in poor countries can access world-class courses. Adults can upskill for new careers. Learning can be self-paced and personalized.
- **Harmful:** Those without Internet are left further behind – the digital divide widens. Online courses require self-discipline; many people start but don't finish. Quality varies; some content is incorrect or biased. Credentialing is still tied to traditional institutions, so online courses may not help with jobs.

**Bias:** Content may reflect Western perspectives (e.g., history taught from Eurocentric view). Platform design may assume high digital literacy, leaving behind older adults or those with less experience. Language barriers – most content is in English.

### Case Study 3: Gig Economy Apps

**The innovation:** Uber, DoorDash, TaskRabbit, Fiverr – platforms that connect workers with customers for short-term tasks.

**Intended purpose:** Provide flexible work opportunities, allow people to earn money on their own schedule, match supply and demand efficiently.

**Unintended effects:**
- **Beneficial:** Income for people who need flexibility (students, parents, those with disabilities). Low barrier to entry – no resume, no interview. Can work as much or as little as you want.
- **Harmful:** Workers are classified as independent contractors, not employees, so they lack benefits (health insurance, paid time off, unemployment). Income is unpredictable. Algorithmic management – the app decides who gets jobs, can deactivate workers without explanation. No job security, no advancement.

**Bias:** Algorithms may rate workers unfairly (e.g., Uber's rating system – one bad passenger can ruin a driver's rating). Facial recognition for identity verification may fail for darker skin, locking them out of work. Language barriers may prevent some from using the app effectively.



## Module 11 Summary

### Beneficial and Harmful Effects
- People create computing innovations; effects are often unanticipated
- A single effect can be both beneficial and harmful depending on perspective
- Computing advances have revolutionized medicine, engineering, communications, arts
- Innovations can be used in unintended ways (WWW, targeted ads, machine learning)
- Harmful impacts on society, economy, culture are possible
- Programmers should try to anticipate misuse, but can't predict everything
- Rapid sharing amplifies effects beyond control

### Digital Divide
- Digital divide = differing access to devices and Internet based on socioeconomic, geographic, demographic factors
- Affects individuals and groups
- Raises issues of equity, access, influence globally and locally
- Can be addressed by individuals, organizations, governments

### Computing Bias
- Computing innovations can reflect human biases through biased algorithms or biased data
- Examples: facial recognition, hiring algorithms, predictive policing, healthcare, criminal justice
- Bias can be embedded at all levels of development
- Programmers should actively work to reduce bias through diverse teams, auditing, testing, transparency
- Bias is not just in code – it's in who builds technology and who it's designed for
