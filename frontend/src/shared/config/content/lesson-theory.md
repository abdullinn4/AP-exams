## Topic 1.1: Scalars and Vectors in One Dimension

In physics, we aim to describe the universe using precise measurements. To do this, we
categorize physical quantities into two main types based on whether or not their direction
matters: scalars and vectors.

### Scalars vs. Vectors

Many physical quantities, such as time, temperature, and mass, are described completely
by a single value (a number) and its corresponding unit. For instance, you might measure a
mass of $6  kg$ or a temperature of $30\degree C$. These are called scalar quantities. A scalar can
be positive, negative, or zero, but it does not have a spatial direction.
However, many other quantities have a directional quality and cannot be described by
a single number alone. To describe the motion of a car, for example, you must specify not
only how fast it is moving (speed), but also the direction in which it is moving.

Vector Quantity: A quantity that has both a magnitude (size) and a direction. 

Magnitude: The ”size” or length of a vector. Magnitude is always positive or zero;
it can never be negative.

**Scalar Examples** 
Distance, Speed 
Mass, Time 
Energy, Temperature 

**Vector Examples**
Displacement, Velocity
Magnetic Fields, Acceleration
Force, Momentum

To distinguish vectors from scalars in our writing and diagrams, we use specific conventions:

### Visual Modeling

We represent vectors graphically as **arrows**.

• The length of the arrow is proportional to the vector’s magnitude.

• The arrowhead points in the direction of the vector quantity.

![Blank diagram.svg](/lesson-images/Blank_diagram.svg)

### **Symbolic Notation**

In equations, vectors are identified by an arrow placed above the variable.

• $\vec{v}$ or $\vec{A}$ represents a vector.

• $v$ or $A$   (without the arrow) represents a scalar or the magnitude of that vector.

*Note: In your handwritten work, it is vital to draw arrows over all vector symbols. We often
use both $\vec{A}$ and $A$ in the same problem, and they represent different concepts.*

### Vector Properties and Operations

Two vectors are considered **equal** if they have the same magnitude and direction, regardless
of their starting points.

**Vector Addition (Resultants)**

The sum of two vectors is called the resultant vector. There are two primary ways to add
vectors graphically. Let’s consider them on the example below.

How to add two vectors $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ to get their vector sum $\vec{A} + \vec{B}$ ?

![Blank diagram (1).svg](/lesson-images/Blank_diagram_(1).svg)

Tip-to-Tail Rule: Place the tail of the second vector at the tip of the first. The
resultant points from the start of the first to the end of the last.

1. Draw $\vec{A}$
2. Place the tail of $\vec{B}$
 at the tip of $\vec{A}$
3. Draw an arrow from the tail of A to the tip of B. This is vector $\vec{A} + \vec{B}$

![Blank diagram (2).svg](/lesson-images/Blank_diagram_(2).svg)

Parallelogram Rule: Place the tails of two vectors together; the resultant is the
diagonal of the parallelogram formed by them.

1. Draw $\vec{A}$
2. Place the tail of $\vec{B}$
 at the tail of $\vec{A}$
3. Form the parallelogram
4. Find the diagonal of the parallelogram 
formed by $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$, which goes from the common tail. This is vector $\vec{A} + \vec{B}$

![Blank diagram (3).svg](/lesson-images/Blank_diagram_(3).svg)

### Scalar Multiplication and Subtraction

Scalar Multiplication: Multiplying a vector $\vec{A}$ by a positive scalar $c$ changes the
magnitude to $cA$ but keeps the direction the same.

![Blank diagram (4).svg](/lesson-images/Blank_diagram_(4).svg)

Vector Subtraction: Subtraction is treated as the addition of a negative vector:

$$
\vec{A} - \vec{B} = \vec{A} + (-\vec{B})
$$

![Blank diagram (5).svg](/lesson-images/Blank_diagram_(5).svg)

The vector $−\vec{A}$ has the same magnitude as $\vec{A}$ but points in the exactly opposite

direction.

### Vectors in One-Dimensional Motion

In one-dimensional (1D) motion, operating with vectors **graphically can be inconvenient**.

Instead, we impose a coordinate system—an artificial grid that allows for quantitative

measurements.

In 1D motion, we typically use a single axis (the x-axis). The ”beauty” of the coordinate

system is how it simplifies vector math:

- Direction via Signs: We use positive (+) and negative (−) signs to denote opposite

directions.

- Components: Because the sign completely describes the direction along the axis,

vector notation (the arrow) is not required for components along an axis.

![Blank diagram (6).svg](/lesson-images/Blank_diagram_(6).svg)

When determining a vector sum in 1D, opposite directions are denoted by opposite signs.
**This allows us to use standard algebraic addition**. Let’s look at the example.

General Vector Definition:

$$
\vec{v} = \vec{v_0}+\vec{a}t 
$$

1D Component Form (Used for Calculation):

$$
v_x = v_{0x} + a_xt
$$
